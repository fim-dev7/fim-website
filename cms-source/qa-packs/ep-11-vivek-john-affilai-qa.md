# What does it mean to "sell before you build"?

slug: sell-before-you-build

answer: It's the YC mantra John says reworked his mind: "the best thing is just always to launch, put yourself out there, see, sell before you build, see who's actually like interested in your product before you just waste time building out a whole pitch deck trying to raise money, like building out a perfect MVP."

John calls it the number one thing YC reworked in him. The point is to launch first and see who's actually interested before wasting time on "a whole pitch deck trying to raise money" or "a perfect MVP." College, he says, taught the opposite — it tries to make you think about "what's your business model" or "what's your 7 slide deck of like your business idea," which matter when you're fundraising but not when you're truly launching a startup. The real job is to "figure out the pain point you're actually solving." If someone doesn't get into YC, John's biggest takeaway is to "really think about like selling before you build, like really take that to heart" — "it blows my mind how simple the idea is, but like it works."

# How do you know if you're actually solving a real pain point?

slug: how-to-validate-startup-idea

answer: John's test is payment: "the easiest way to figure out if you're solving a pain point is if people pay for it, and how quickly they're willing to pay for it." Surveys and waitlists tell you almost nothing; a customer paying $200 per seat tells you everything.

John's college mistake was sending out surveys and creating waitlists to manufacture demand. His realization was that "they are not necessarily doing it because they want to buy the product. They might just be doing it because they're your friend." He contrasts asking someone how much they'd pay versus "them paying me $200 per seat" — "that is like a night and day juxtaposition," where the former "doesn't give you actually any actual information" but the latter turns into "wow I have a customer paying me $600 for three people, how many of these customers are out there." If you're spending time business-modeling instead, "I think you're focusing on the wrong problem."

# How do I do customer discovery as a founder?

slug: how-to-do-customer-discovery

answer: For Vivek and John, discovery meant getting to payment as fast as possible. The lesson from a botched deal: "getting to like the point of how much are you willing to pay as fast as possible in like the relationship" is "the biggest thing you should follow," because free pilots told them nothing real.

Their hard lesson came from a company that "kind of led us on... dragging their feet for like a month or two," signed an NDA, said "we're so excited to build this," and then "completely ghosted us" after John "followed up after like four or five times." The realization: "the fact that they weren't paying for this or like willing to... go through legal processes" should have been an immediate signal. They'd been doing free pilots, "which we thought would help like actually understand if... my customer actually wants the product," but "it turns out that they did not really care, they were just either being nice or just thought it was like a cool tool they could try out." The fix is to push toward "how much are you willing to pay as fast as possible."

# Should I do free pilots or paid pilots with early customers?

slug: paid-pilots-vs-free-pilots

answer: Paid, every time. John says "a big lesson we Learned is that you should do paid pilots." Their free pilots felt like validation but weren't — customers "did not really care, they were just either being nice or just thought it was like a cool tool they could try out."

The founders explain why free pilots misled them. They ran free pilots thinking it would tell them whether the customer truly wanted the product, but free interest is cheap — people will take a free tool out of politeness or curiosity. The signal they actually needed was money: "if you are [solving a real pain point], they will be willing to throw any amount of money at you for it, even if it doesn't work." That insight directly shaped how they ran the first real deal, where the customer paid and signed a contract before the product was even close to finished.

# How do you know it's time to pivot?

Vivek and John pivoted off an affiliate-relationship-management idea after a company "led us on," signed an NDA, then ghosted — realizing the not-paying was the signal. They moved to compliance because it was "a more specific problem that we could solve."

Their original idea was helping affiliate marketers understand and grow their affiliates "like a flywheel." The pivot trigger was a company that dragged its feet "for like a month or two," signed an NDA, said it was excited, and then ghosted after "four or five" follow-ups. "We should have almost immediately realized that the fact that they weren't paying for this... we were just kind of clinging onto hope." They moved to compliance because "it was like a more specific problem that we could solve" — across all the people they talked to, affiliate marketing had "so many random different problems," but with compliance they knew "the No. 1 thing" financial companies care about is making sure affiliates "are actually saying the right things, following the guidelines." It also fit John's experience in the space.

# What does Affil.ai actually do?

slug: what-does-affilai-do

answer: Affil.ai builds AI compliance for financial affiliate marketing. As John explains, "we help companies basically make sure that everything that they write about is meeting credit card marketing guidelines" — for example, that a creator advertises the Chase Sapphire Preferred or the Amex Gold correctly.

John lays out the problem concretely. They "help a content creator make sure that they're advertising the Chase Sapphire Preferred or the Amex Gold correctly, because... if they don't market it correctly, then the banks could be very angry at them" — banks "could take back the commissions they get from getting people to sign up for cards, or they could kick them out of the programs cause it's technically false advertising." John knew the pain point firsthand: he has "43 credit cards," tried becoming an affiliate of a credit card program "back in like 2021, 2022," and later "launched the entire affiliate program" at a travel startup, learning quirks like companies requiring you "to basically say miles instead of points" or that statement credits "have to be advertised as up to" rather than flat numbers. Coming in "with so much compliance experience" became the wedge into the space.
