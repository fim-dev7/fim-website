# What is Order Editing?

slug: what-is-order-editing
answer: Order Editing is a software company that online shoppers use to make changes to their order after they buy something online, while online retailers pay a monthly subscription to use the software. Hamish McKay describes it as giving customers a "Grace period" to make changes — just like you can do on Amazon — but brought to Shopify specifically.

The way it works is mostly their own technology, their own IP, that guarantees the retailer's system doesn't get the order until Order Editing says so. Some systems they've integrated with or worked through API calls, but the majority of the time they've built the IP themselves. Hamish calls it "very simple but very effective."

# How does Order Editing make money for brands?

slug: how-order-editing-monetizes-post-purchase
answer: Beyond saving customer-service time, Order Editing monetizes the post-purchase moment. When a brand gives someone a Grace period, that customer can also buy more products, so the checkout total is no longer fixed — the brand has a whole 30 minutes or an hour to convince customers to add another item.

Hamish says they've got brands making like $500,000 a year or $1 million a year by monetizing their post purchase, getting customers to go from two to three items after the initial commitment — breaking up the purchase into smaller chunks. He frames the customer-service savings around the example of a brand doing $50 million a year shipping 50,000 packages a month, where about 2% of customers make a mistake at checkout, which works out to roughly 1,000 emails a month — close to a full-time employee spending every minute of an 8 hour day making changes for orders.

# How do I find my first customers?

slug: how-to-find-first-customers
answer: For Hamish, the first two clients came directly from a single LinkedIn post. He'd spent about six months marketing the wrong positioning and hadn't made a single dollar, then posted showing how a brand could email a customer after they buy and offer 20% off for the next half hour. They posted it immediately and signed their first two clients off the back of it.

The first client was a nine figure brand, and they signed Nike directly after — who are now investors in the business. Hamish also made sure those early customers were massive brands on purpose: the first UK client is Opoli, a 100 million pound brand, because that makes people ask "how did you get that client so early" and is interesting to follow and engage with on social.

# How did Order Editing find product-market fit?

slug: how-to-find-product-market-fit
answer: The turning point was discovering they'd been positioned wrong. Hamish had started the business thinking the most valuable, interesting part was saving brands time and money on customer service — the editing of the order, removing the volume of emails. They centered their marketing on that for about six months and hadn't made a single dollar.

Then a LinkedIn post showing post-purchase upsell — "there's space in your package for another item, get 20% off for the next half hour" — landed their first two clients immediately. As Hamish puts it, "the veil was lifted" and what they'd thought their marketing should be positioned around was wrong. The real value was monetizing the post-purchase moment, with brands making $500,000 to $1 million a year from it.

# How do I price my product when I don't have customers yet?

slug: how-to-price-without-customers
answer: Hamish's approach was to keep testing. Order Editing changed its pricing almost every two months. They launched it at like four hundred dollars a month, then thought "can we sell it at 500," found out they could, then 600 — gradually finding out what people's willingness to pay was.

His point: when you start a company and put a price on it, what's the pricing based off? It's very hard to know until you've got clients and can see what type of value they get and how they perceive it. He ties this to the broader idea that early on no one knows who you are, so you should test pricing, messaging and the structure of your business model "without asking for too much permission."

# How do I test different value propositions early on?

slug: how-to-test-value-propositions
answer: Hamish's advice is to take advantage of the fact that no one knows who you are and no one cares about your messaging yet. If you've got 10 sales calls in a month, you can take every single sales call differently — and that's probably the best way to spend your time, rather than selling the exact same way and assuming it's correct.

You may close relatively fewer deals doing lots of different pitches, since some won't land, but you're far more likely to find the best pitch that you can then use ongoing. That freedom extends from messaging to pricing to the structure of your business model — a liberating period where, as Hamish says, there are just no expectations and you can genuinely do whatever you want.

# Should I bootstrap or raise venture capital?

slug: bootstrap-vs-venture
answer: Hamish deliberately didn't raise venture capital. His internal goal was to "do VC back growth without the VC backing" — and part of the reason was not wanting the pressure associated with venture capital, or other people's expectations influencing the business and the mental fog that might create.

The honest trade-off he names: by building in public and being open about all the numbers, "we traded off intra capital pressure for just general public pressure" — laying it all back on by involving the whole world in what they're doing. He did take small checks from three influential angel investors in the industry, losing maybe 3% of equity, but they brought in three or four hundred thousand dollars of annual recurring revenue and added millions to the company's valuation.

# Why did Hamish McKay build Order Editing in public?

slug: how-to-build-in-public
answer: When Hamish decided he wanted to be a founder at 20, one of the first things he did was start posting on LinkedIn — before he had a company — because he thought it would increase his likelihood of becoming a founder. In Order Editing's first year, building in public was "100% the most viral way to grow."

He knew they'd close more revenue if they were candid about what their revenue was, because being open maximizes feelings like FOMO, inspiration and affinity — it's a lot easier to root for someone when you know exactly where they're at in their journey. The catch: once you cross $1 million it immediately becomes not that interesting, and the openness creates a huge mental burden, with people misinterpreting reflective posts and reaching out worried he seems stressed.

# How do I grow a founder-led brand on LinkedIn?

slug: how-to-grow-a-founder-led-brand
answer: Hamish's core advice is that every time you post, the next post should be incrementally better — constantly analyzing what landed, what didn't and how to improve it. He borrows the YouTuber logic: post a video a week and in two years you'll be successful, because by your hundredth video, constantly getting better, there's no way you're not writing top 1% content.

He says you don't necessarily need to post every day; what's more important is not diluting your content or your thoughts, and posting when you actually have something meaningful to say. His reassurance: "if it's a shit post no one will see it anyways" — the algorithm hides the bad stuff and surfaces the good. For LinkedIn specifically, photos perform better, make sure the hook doesn't get cut off with three dots on a mobile view, and respect the reader so a complete stranger with no context could read and enjoy it.

# What's it like to build a medium sized company on purpose?

slug: building-medium-sized-company
answer: Both Hamish and his co-founder Carol decided before they started the business that they were going to grow what could only be described as a medium sized company — there was no ambition to build a massive organization. A lot of how they think about scaling is driven by speed and medium-term ambitions rather than longer-term ones.

This is why Order Editing currently exists as an add-on via Shopify only and has decided not to expand to other e-commerce platforms. Hamish admits you could sell to a brand like Nike for hundreds of thousands of dollars a year, and that building for the long term would require diversifying onto other platforms. But that would take two or three years to build, get to market and grow awareness — whereas spending those two to three years investing in Shopify could get them to 10 or 12 million ARR. It's nonsensical, he says, when you're not looking to be the operator of your business in five years time.

# How do I avoid burning out as a first-time founder?

slug: how-to-avoid-founder-burnout
answer: Hamish's most valuable lesson is that, like anything in life, you get better at it over time. As a first-time founder he'd really struggle mentally — stressing about hiring or firing people, building a team, making big decisions he'd never made before — and it would affect his energy and his output.

He wishes future him could have been in his ear saying it's all good, it'll be imperfect the first time but the next time gets better and better, and eventually you'll be really good at it — these are all puzzles to solve. He warns against existential dread about where the company is heading, because of course you've never managed a business like that. Instead, just focus on what it feels like to manage three people, then try four, then five, "and suddenly you'll find you'll be at the end of the year and you're like holy shit I'm managing 10 people."

# How does Hamish McKay think about scaling Order Editing to 5 million in AR?

slug: how-to-scale-to-revenue-goal
answer: Hamish describes going through a period of anxiety and then clarity. Order Editing will finish the year doing about two and a half in ARR — roughly 3x on last year — and he was freaking out about how to go from 2.5 to 7.5 in 12 months, calling it actually insane.

The math drove the clarity: even at a 50% close rate without changing the ACV they'd have to pitch around 2,000 people next year — eight deals a day — and it could be 3,000 deals to acquire in the pretty small market of the Shopify ecosystem. So the strategy update became about building a product team to make room for ideas, and a strong account-management and client-management layer so it's easy, fast and efficient to sell more products to existing customers.

# How do you balance grinding on a startup with enjoying your 20s?

slug: how-to-balance-startup-and-life
answer: Hamish admits it's probably not super balanced, and he balances it by way of acceptance. He decided at 20 that he wanted this by the time he was 25, reverse engineered what he'd need to do, and framed it as "what if I just slam life hard for five years" and then have the rest of his life for a deep imbalance toward the things that give him the most joy.

Last year he was averaging 30 hours a week on work meetings and working from 7 to 11 or 7 to midnight every day, including weekends. Now he has 10 hours of meetings a week because he's got a team, still works 7 to 11 but knocks off at 5 on Friday and doesn't work weekends. He calls building a company a very selfish endeavour, where even when he's out, the majority of the time he's still thinking about work and not present.

# What is Hamish McKay's most controversial startup hot take?

slug: founder-hot-take
answer: "Fuck growing to be as big as possible" — for certain people. Hamish's point is that different types of people want different types of businesses, and people should build businesses that they want to operate and want to be in.

He thinks it's actually quite sad and unfortunate that there isn't more of a community or public thesis by founders that you can be intentional about how you grow your business and predefine the parameters of what would give your life the most joy. Some people just want to make yogurt, some want to make alcohol, some want to grow a business and sell it in a few years — all super fair paths. But the media glamorizes huge-valuation, gigantic companies and billion dollar aspiration, when going down that path is very stressful, takes basically your entire 20s and potentially your 30s, and isn't even guaranteed.

# What does the "verbs not nouns" idea mean to Hamish McKay?

slug: verbs-not-nouns-hamish
answer: Hamish references an Oscar Wilde quote he loves — that "we're verbs not nouns" — meaning you should never describe yourself as, for example, "I'm a founder." Instead it's "I'm just doing the founder thing at the moment."

He applies it to his own future: for the next five years he's going to be writing, but he's not a writer — he's just writing at the moment. His post-exit dream is to spend his late 20s to 30s, hopefully financially free, doing a postgraduate masters in creative writing, tackling the scary challenge of writing a novel, and maybe making income telling stories on the internet through a YouTube channel. Part of being human, he says, is to evolve over time, and he likes never putting himself in a box.
