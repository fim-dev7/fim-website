# What is a wallet led marketing platform?

slug: what-is-wallet-led-marketing-platform
answer: Brian Pham describes Litecard as a "wallet led marketing platform and wallet, meaning Apple and Google Wallet" — the wallets people use every day for bank cards and boarding passes, repurposed as a media channel for brands and retailers.

Brian's pitch starts from a marketing problem: it's tough for a brand or retailer to reach an end customer. SMS reads as spam and many countries don't send SMS for marketing, email "doesn't go into your inbox anymore. It goes into your social, you know, promotions, inbox," and channels like YouTube, Google and Facebook are "becoming more expensive by design." His conclusion: "As a brand and a retailer, you would definitely have a problem in the next 10 or 20 years around where to spend your media to acquire and retain a customer." Litecard's answer is to use Apple and Google Wallet — which people already use for bank cards and boarding passes — as that new media channel.

# Why does Litecard sell to agencies instead of just brands?

slug: why-litecard-sells-through-agencies
answer: Because the brands often aren't the ones executing their own marketing. Brian says "a lot of the times, maybe the brand and the retailers aren't even executing their own strategy. It's the agencies they work with," so Litecard built a platform "made for agencies as well as these brands."

Brian found that even leading brands who loved the idea "made us work with the agencies that they work with anyway." The first real media agency relationship came through North Face: "when North Face, we went live in Australia, they said, you have to work with our media agency in South Australia, that's an external agency." His team then worked to compress the workflow — "how can we make this process like out of 100 clicks, ten clicks" — and that shaped how Litecard works with every agency since, learning that some are very technical and do the integration themselves, some are media-strategy focused and just want the tools, and some want both.

# How did Litecard reduce technical friction in its product?

slug: how-litecard-reduced-technical-friction
answer: By evolving the product until it needed almost no coding. Brian says early objections were about "too much, sort of coding and technical integrations," but now the whole add-to-wallet-and-scan flow "takes sort of like 1 to 2 minutes. You don't have to do any integrations."

Brian says the biggest early objection was that "providing a product into the mobile wallet and then using it for advertising, at a beginning of our journey there was too much, sort of coding and technical integrations needed." As they worked with more retailers and brands, they evolved it to require less. He gives the example that a brand like L'Oréal, Mars or Jack Daniels could "go to a Chemist Warehouse or Dan's Murphy and buy like" a hundred thousand barcodes, load them into the platform, customise the journey, and a customer could click a social media ad, add it to their wallet, and "come and scan in demo fees immediately." His framing: objections "went from like technical and security to, like, now there's nothing really to worry about. And you can do it yourself."

# How do you decide which international market to enter?

slug: how-to-expand-startup-internationally
answer: Brian's team ran "pretty deep analytics across the whole world" after 12 to 18 months in market — segmenting customers, comparing messaging costs per region, and feeding it into an algorithm to find regions where Google and Apple Wallet were already available with users present.

Brian explains they broke customers "into different data segments" by brand and retail sub-segments — consumer packaged goods, fast moving goods, fashion, grocery, pharmacy — and categorized that across the world. Then they studied "how much is an SMS, how much is an email, how much is it push notifications in each region" and "fed that into an algorithm" to flag a strong region where "Google and Apple Wallet is available there and there are like users in the region already." They also asked practical network questions — "do we know people there? Do we have shop for agency connections?" — and copied what worked in Australia "as much as possible, taking into account like different things like price, sales cycles, buying habits of these enterprise customers."

# Why is Australia a good place to test a startup?

slug: why-australia-is-a-startup-testing-ground
answer: Brian says Australia "has been a release point for a lot of us and UK based companies," Apple and Google Wallet's latest releases "after the US come to Australia first, like a majority of the time," and it's a crowded market — so winning there signals a strong chance globally.

Brian argues Australians are "very advanced and forward thinking in the way they think about mobile technology and shopping behaviors." He points to market density: "In terms of point of sale companies, there are 30 or 40, 50 in the market when the US there might be less than ten, but they have ten times the amount of population." Because it's "a very crowded market in Australia, especially for marketing platforms, fintech, new innovation," his hypothesis is that "if you can come out on top of a lot of people trying to sell similar things, then most likely, you know, from a data perspective, you have a strong chance across the rest of the world as well."

# Was there a market that looked perfect but turned out harder than expected?

slug: market-that-looked-perfect-but-was-hard
answer: The Philippines. Brian says almost everyone told them it was a great market because shopping habits there are "very similar to the shopping habits of people in America," but they "didn't know where to start" until a network introduction to a brand family opened the door.

Brian recounts that the Philippines looked great — rich in multiple cultures, plenty of coupons and digital transformation, brands trying to expand — "but we figured out that, like, we didn't know where to start." The breakthrough came through their network: "someone introduced us to a brand family within the Philippines, and they own the distribution rights of over 100 brands in the Philippines," plus their own shopping centres and their own marketing agency. They formed an alliance and the brand family "saw, you know, the potential immediately and became a partner in the region." His takeaway: "I think we're glad that we keep an open mind and we're lucky enough to still be agile to make these decisions."

# Why did Litecard open its first European office in Amsterdam?

slug: why-litecard-opened-amsterdam-office
answer: Because partners and customers were already in the region and Europe was too large to serve remotely. Brian says "the reason why we went into the European market is because we had partners and customers" already there, and they "found that it was best to have a person that was on the ground."

Brian describes Europe as "an amazing country always because such rich culture, there's a lot of people in the region as well," giving them "high sort of economic variables" to understand. But because the region is so large, "it was hard for us to sort of, you know, speak the same language as all the different like, agencies inside the Netherlands, Switzerland, Germany, Spain, Italy and so on." Having someone on the ground solved that. He frames expansion as experimentation: "with all experiments it's, you know, one variable at a time and you sort of measure it and understand it."

# How do I do customer discovery?

slug: how-to-do-customer-discovery
answer: Brian's approach was sitting with agencies and brands and reshaping the product around what each needed. Working with North Face's media agency, he asked "how can we make this process like out of 100 clicks, ten clicks," then learned that agencies differ — some technical, some media-strategy focused, some both.

Brian says it came from "having all those conversations after working with the North Face and then it moves on to all the other agencies as well. And just like understanding, you know, what each agency needs and how they differ from each other." Some agencies "are very technical and they like to do a lot of the integration themselves," some "are very sort of media strategy consulting focused, and they want to do mainly the execution and have the tools and not have to do any engineering," and "there are some agencies I want to do both." That listening also revealed the underlying customer pain — that brands like L'Oréal "run a promotion and have no idea who's seen the promotion and what the customer demographic is."

# How do I find my first 10 customers?

slug: how-to-find-first-customers
answer: Brian's first agency channel came from a single brand requirement that he turned into a repeatable motion. North Face made Litecard work with their agency; when that agency wanted to sell it to its own clients, Brian asked how to make that "a default scenario" — one sale becoming ten.

Brian found that after talking to many brands and retailers, even leading brands "made us work with the agencies that they work with anyway," and "the agency thought it was a really good idea and they wanted to sell it to their clients as well." His reaction: "that's great, like we just did with one. So like another ten sales. So how can we create that as like a default? You know, scenario." He also leaned on the Australian agency ecosystem, where "once you work with an agency in Australia that does really well, they usually talk with an agency in like Thailand or Singapore, Hong Kong" — global agencies with alliances across the world that helped Litecard expand.

# What would Brian Pham do differently if he started Litecard again?

slug: what-brian-would-do-differently
answer: Study the data faster. Brian says "if I was to do it all over again, I would decide and study the data faster" — instead of getting excited about the technology first and figuring out the data later, he'd ask how big and how global the problem is from day one.

Brian admits that "as most founders do," he gets "excited about the technology and try and figure out the data after." If he did it again, "I would bring that back as early as possible" and ask "in terms of money dollar value, where am I solving a problem? How big is the problem? Can it be bigger and is a global problem or not?" He believes that "if I just ask myself those questions at the very beginning of the journey, may be able to speed it up 2 or 3 times." Even so, he frames it as a fine line "between being an optimist and a realist," and that "everything that we learned along the way shaped our business as we are today."

# How does AI fit into a retail media platform?

slug: how-ai-fits-into-retail-media
answer: For Brian, "AI revolves a lot around data, and it's about how much you can gather." Litecard uses an AI-driven customer onboarding journey for partners, and looks at AI to speed up building brand experiences — turning "20 clicks" into "30s in one click."

Brian says the data a brand gathers from a customer "has been what's influenced us the most," because a brand like L'Oréal might run a promotion with "no idea who's seen the promotion and what the customer demographic is." Litecard's platform lets customers click a promotion, fill out details and get an offer, which helps the brand understand buying habits. On their own product, "Our whole like customer onboarding journey is like" AI-driven — "if you're a partner, you sign up with LiteCard... any question you would ever have. The AI will be able to answer it." He also wants AI to speed up building a brand experience, asking "what if it took 30s in one click instead of like 20 clicks."
