# How did Caroline Tran land her first merchant with no brand and no connections?
slug: how-to-find-first-customers
answer: Hello Clever's first merchant came from a Facebook group: the CEO of one of the largest telco companies in Tasmania asked who did return payments, and Caroline reached out — "Ashley we do have something for you" — and signed him up with "no brand" while still building the first product.

Caroline had "zero connections in banking or merchant e commerce." The first merchant was based in Tasmania and "posted a a questions on a Facebook group that I was part of," asking who was doing return payments because he ran one of the largest telco companies in Tasmania and wanted real-time payments instead of Visa or Mastercard. Caroline reached out directly, brought in co-founder Gavin and a young salesperson, and he "literally just signed up with us as the first merchant" back when "we're literally has no brand." Since then that first merchant kept giving feedback on how to improve the product, which Caroline says was "so valuable" coming from a business of that size. Her takeaway: "it's lucky but also we we just go for it you got nothing to lose."

# How do you do customer discovery and find the real problem to solve?
slug: how-to-do-customer-discovery
answer: For Caroline the real problem surfaced from conversations, not planning. An advisor mentioned "return payments," she'd wrongly assumed "payment is real time," and market research then showed no one was doing real-time consumer-to-business payments in Australia.

Caroline says when they pivoted, "I had a chat with one of our advisors and he mentioned about return payments." She'd assumed payment was real time; he told her it wasn't. That conversation — the advisor was "a global chief compliance officer at a really big fintech company they're unicorn" — sent them into market research, where they "realised actually yeah no one was doing real time payments in Australia specifically on the consumer to business payment." That gap became the product. As of today, she says, "we continue to see problems as we go… as we find problem we have to build a solution for it, and that's how we identify gaps."

# How do you validate a startup idea before building it?
slug: how-to-validate-startup-idea
answer: Caroline's hard-won rule is research demand first. After building products that "no one's buying," she now asks who the customers are, whether there's demand, and whether they'll pay — before building anything.

Caroline says the belief she got wrong was building products fast: "if you build a product it doesn't necessarily that it will commercialize." With "talented engineers based in Vietnam" they could ship quickly, but "in the past there's occasions where we just build product and without realizing oh actually no one's buying it." Now "what we're doing right now is doing a lot of research before we actually start building something" — asking "whose are the customers right, do we have demand for this right, will they pay for this right." She frames it as avoiding "wasting times and resources."

# How do you know your MVP is ready to launch in a regulated industry?
slug: how-to-know-mvp-is-ready
answer: You don't wait for perfect. Caroline says in fintech "you can never wait for it to be hundred percent complete or like hundred percent perfect" — that's "the definition of MVP." Build something small, get merchant feedback, commercialize, and fine-tune as you go.

Caroline says MVP "really means something — you need to start building something small and getting feedback from merchants, start to commercialize it." If you keep building more and more, "you don't know whether it's good fit your merchants anyway." Every new product "it's always MVP right, and then we continue to get feedback and fine tune it as we go." She warns against the opposite: "if I've just start to build everything in one go then I can't converge, it's just too much," and "sometimes you build something that's actually very unnecessary."

# How did Caroline figure out what to charge her first customers?
slug: how-to-price-first-customer
answer: For the first merchant she gave a price that was reasonable rather than cheap, then researched how competitors charged. Merchants themselves supplied data points, and Hello Clever now has "a really well structured commercial model."

Caroline says when they signed the first merchant, "we gave them a really… I wouldn't say like more cheaper but it's just more like reasonable." To set pricing she advises speaking to people in the industry and researching "how your competitor is charging, what is their business model." Merchants themselves supplied data points — "actually your competitor charge me this much." Today, she says, "we do have a really well structured commercial model," with a channel manager who has "really strong background in payment," so "as soon as we put out a proposal the merchants like yep this is perfect."

# How did Hello Clever go from local to processing payments across 20 countries?
slug: how-to-expand-internationally
answer: The growth flip came from a strategy Caroline calls "multi Geo, multi infrastructure and multi product." After 18 months hyper-focused on Australia, Hello Clever built banking relationships globally — going from about two to roughly 60 — and got its Australian Financial Services License.

Caroline says exponential growth "really happens when we started our strategy around multi Geo, multi infrastructure and multi product." They started in Australia in 2022 and "been hyper focused to Australia until actually early 2024, so that was 18 months." The catalyst to go global was risk: "for you to depend on one single bank to grow your business is not ideal — what if they say no to you?" So from mid-2024 they pursued "more and more banking relationships globally," strengthened compliance, and got their Australian Financial Services License. The result: "we've got about 60 banking relationships globally… instead of like two 24 months ago," with banks "in Japan, Philippines… the US and banks in Australia," and the Philippines as "our strongest market."

answer: A large merchant pulled out at 11 PM — the general manager called to say they were shutting down Australia. Caroline turned it around by recruiting that GM, Rush Kelly, who's now been with Hello Clever nearly three years leading the Singapore regional hub.

Rather than walk away, she called the GM — Rush Kelly — the next day: "hey this is us right, come join." He started immediately and "has been like with us… nearly three years, really happy based in Singapore, our regional hub," now leading "a team of 10 people." She notes "some vcs told me that like you have Rush Kelly you don't need anyone else." Her reflection: "if I didn't call him that night he probably would gone somewhere else."

# How do you build a strong team culture across a global startup?
slug: how-to-build-company-culture
answer: Caroline's approach is empowering people over micromanaging, plus rituals that keep a 70-person global team connected: a Monday all-hands called Clever Together, a Clever Pitch session where anyone presents their view of the company, and Clever Ideas for feature proposals.

Caroline says she and her co-founder "are all about empowering people to do their jobs — we don't go to people and micromanage them at all, we trust them to do the best of their job." With "nearly 70 employees globally," "five offices," and people who "speak like 10 languages," she gets everyone together "literally every Monday" for an all-hands "we call clever together." There's "Clever Pitch," where "whether you're an engineer… whether it's your CFO CTO," people "show us your perspective about Hello Clever," and "Clever Ideas," where anyone can "propose a feature." She adds in-person connection through a comedy trip and regular Team Upsides, because "you can't really replace the in person interactions." On hiring she says "no one works for a startup for the base salary — it's all about they align with the mission and wanna grow with the business."

# How do you pivot when you're running out of money?
slug: how-to-pivot-without-losing-team
answer: Hello Clever pivoted from a consumer financial app to merchant payments because they ran out of money and consumer products were hard to fund in Australia. Caroline picked a product that could "immediately commercialize and start making revenue."

Caroline says the original idea was "building a financial apps for Gen z's and millennials," and "we got like thousand of users that downloading our apps," but "for us to be able to scale this product it requires a lot of capital and Australia is not a market for consumers consumer product in general." The forcing function was money: "we need to switch or pivot it because we run out of money — either we need more capital or we pivot it." With interest rates rising in 2021–2022 and "pitching a consumer product in Australia" being hard, "we quickly pivoted and launched a product that immediately commercialize and start making revenue." She frames pivoting as normal: "when you started a business it actually takes a couple of times to get it right."

# When should you leave your corporate job to start a company?
slug: when-to-quit-job-to-start-company
answer: For Caroline the trigger was Covid in 2021 — "sitting in my room in the middle of Covid" made her question life and decide "this is like my time to do something different." Her practical advice: make sure you have enough money first, and she wishes she'd started earlier.

Caroline says the moment the corporate path stopped fitting was "2021, when I was sitting in my room in the middle of Covid and I realized what I'm doing here — Covid makes you question life." She'd always wanted to build a business, "and covid just made everything happened." Asked what she'd tell her 2020 self still in a 9-to-5, she says "definitely make sure that you have enough money to do it," plus "look after your health and have fun while you're at it." And the regret: "one thing I wish I could have done is that I would have started earlier."

# How do you test demand for a new product before building it?
slug: how-to-test-demand-before-launch
answer: Caroline now insists on validating demand before any build — to the point her co-founder's reflex is "justify the demand." It came from shipping products fast that ultimately "no one's buying."

Caroline says the co-founder dynamic enforces this: "if I go to my co founder right now and tell him to do something, he will come back to me be like — justify the demand." She values that pushback: "it's like having someone that can challenge you and actually pushes you back on certain things." The reason is concrete — building without demand means "wasting his engineer's time." Even Clever AI grew this way: they built one product, then "got feedbacks around merchants that wanting us to do more," and "continue to fine tune it" rather than "build everything in one go."

# What's the most valuable lesson Caroline Tran learned as a founder?
slug: caroline-tran-most-valuable-lesson
answer: Getting the right people in the team. Caroline says her most valuable lesson is building "great management team, great leadership team" with real experience — and accepting that you won't find the right problem right away, so you pivot and adapt as you go.

Caroline says "one of the most valuable lessons for me is getting the right people in the team — great management team, great leadership team who's got experience in doing whether it's payments or b to B sales." The second lesson is to "just go with it," because "we pivoted a couple of times" and "it's not like you will find the right problem right away." She ties it to staying adaptive: "as you can see AI is everything now, so you have to be super focusing on how the market is reacting and how it can fly into your business use case."
